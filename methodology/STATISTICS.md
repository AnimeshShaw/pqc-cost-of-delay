# Statistical analysis

Each analysis below states the **question** it answers, the **method**, the **result** (generated by `scripts/make_results.py`; exact numbers in `results/results.json` and `results/summary.md`), and its **limit**. Posited (invented) assets are excluded from every statistic. All random procedures use fixed seeds and are reproducible.

The model's inputs are ordinal bands, not measurements. The central device is therefore **input-uncertainty propagation**: every input of every asset is re-drawn from a stated range, the whole register is re-scored, and we count how often each conclusion survives. The ranges are listed in `PARAMETERS.md` §2.10 and are themselves assumptions.

## 1. Descriptive comparison of the combined and classical-only rankings

**Question.** On each system, how far does adding the quantum term move the priority order?

**Method.** Kendall's $\tau$ between the combined ranking and a classical-only ranking (Kendall 1938): $\tau=(C-D)/\binom n2$ where $C,D$ count concordant and discordant pairs. $\tau=1$ identical, $0$ unrelated.

**Result (central estimates, GRI optimistic scenario).**

| System | Assets | Pairs ranked differently | Kendall $\tau$ | Quantum-led assets |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MOSIP | 9 | 5 of 36 | 0.72 | 4 |
| Apache Fineract | 9 | 0 of 36 | 1.00 | 1 |
| OpenMRS | 10 | 6 of 45 | 0.73 | 1 |
| Online Boutique (mock) | 7 | 2 of 21 | 0.81 | 0 |

**Limit.** A single point estimate, which is exactly why the next sections exist.

## 2. Does the quantum term move the list more than input noise does?  (signal-to-noise)

**Question.** The quantum term reorders some pairs. But the list also churns simply because two analysts would assign slightly different bands. Which is bigger?

**Method.** On 4,000 perturbed registers per system:

* *signal* = pairs ordered differently by the combined ranking than by the classical-only ranking, **in the same perturbed register**;
* *noise (two analysts)* = pairs ordered differently between the classical-only rankings of **two independent** perturbed registers;
* *noise (versus the central estimate)* = pairs ordered differently between the central classical ranking and one perturbed one.

The ratio signal/noise exceeds 1 if the quantum term moves the list more than uncertainty does. Percentile-bootstrap intervals (Efron 1979) over the draws.

**Result.**

| System | Signal (pairs) | Noise, two analysts | Ratio [95% interval] | Ratio vs central estimate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MOSIP | 5.09 | 3.75 | **1.36** [1.33, 1.38] | 1.98 |
| Apache Fineract | 1.44 | 3.14 | **0.46** [0.44, 0.47] | 0.69 |
| OpenMRS | 4.23 | 10.37 | **0.41** [0.40, 0.42] | 0.52 |
| Online Boutique | 0.29 | 2.14 | **0.14** [0.13, 0.14] | 0.17 |

**Interpretation, stated plainly.** Only for MOSIP, the system with 50-year data, does the quantum term reorder the list more than input uncertainty does. For the other three it moves the list **less** than two analysts' differences would. The claim "the quantum term reshuffles the priority list" is therefore **not** supported in general. What the data do support is narrower: the quantum term changes the *magnitude* of the cost for assets with long lifetimes, and moves *specific* assets decisively (§3), without reordering the list as a whole beyond noise for medium-lifetime systems. Figure: Figure 13 (`python scripts/make_figures.py`).

**Limit.** The ratio depends on the assumed perturbation ranges; narrower ranges would raise it. It compares two reorderings, not their consequences.

## 3. Which individual conclusions survive?  (per-asset probabilities)

**Question.** For a specific asset, how often is it quantum-led, how far does it move, how often does it reach the funded top?

**Method.** 4,000 perturbed registers per system; Wilson score intervals for proportions (Wilson 1927), which behave correctly near 0 and 1, where these probabilities sit.

**Result for the key asset of each system.**

| System / asset | P(quantum-led) [95% Wilson] | In funded top-2: combined vs classical-only | Median rank (5–95%) | P(moves up ≥ 2 places) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| MOSIP / M03 registration packets | 100.0% [99.9, 100] | **54.8%** vs 0.0% | 2 (1–5) | 90.6% |
| OpenMRS / O08 off-site backups | **87.3%** [86.2, 88.3] | 18.7% vs 1.2% | 5 (1–8) | 56.0% |
| Apache Fineract / B06 off-site backups | 64.0% [62.5, 65.5] | 8.0% vs 1.5% | 4 (2–6) | 13.9% |
| Online Boutique / A02 customer PII | 25.7% | 99.4% vs 98.6% | 1 (1–2) | 0.3% |

Probability that the funded top-$k$ **set** differs from classical-only: MOSIP 71%, OpenMRS 45%, Fineract 26%, Online Boutique 2%.

**Interpretation.** The quantum term matters decisively for particular long-lived, quiet, recordable assets (registration packets; backups). For the demo shop it does not.

**Limit.** "Funded top-$k$" uses $k=\mathrm{round}(0.25\,n)$ (2 of 9 or 10 assets here), an arbitrary planning cut; probabilities for other cuts differ.

## 3a. Distribution of agreement between the two rankings

Kendall $\tau$ between the combined and classical-only rankings across the 4,000 draws: MOSIP median 0.72 (5–95%: 0.56–0.89); Fineract 0.94 (0.78–1.00; identical in 24.5% of draws); OpenMRS 0.82 (0.60–0.96); Online Boutique 1.00 (0.90–1.00; identical in 74%).

## 4. What drives the answer?  (global sensitivity analysis)

**Question.** Of the seven uncertain inputs, which explain the uncertainty in an asset's quantum cost of delay?

**Method.** Variance-based global sensitivity analysis (Saltelli et al. 2010) with Sobol' indices (Sobol' 2001): the **first-order** index $S_i$ is the share of output variance explained by input $i$ alone; the **total-order** index $S_{T,i}$ includes its interactions. Estimated with Saltelli's sampling design and Jansen's (1999) estimators on a scrambled Sobol' sequence, $N=4096$ base samples ($N(d+2)=36{,}864$ model runs per asset), with 300 bootstrap resamples for intervals. The output is $\log_{10}$ of the quantum cost of delay. The CRQC date enters as a continuous median year (2031–2046) with the optimistic shape.

**Result.** Shares of variance (first-order; total-order differs by under 0.02 everywhere, i.e. the model is nearly additive in log space):

| Input | MOSIP M03 | Fineract B06 | OpenMRS O08 | Boutique A02 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Loss $V$ | 0.48 | 0.42 | 0.47 | 0.38 |
| Turnover $\tau$ | 0.43 | 0.38 | 0.43 | 0.35 |
| **$V+\tau$** | **0.91** | **0.79** | **0.89** | **0.73** |
| CRQC median year | 0.03 | 0.15 | 0.05 | 0.17 |
| Discount $\rho$ | 0.05 | 0.01 | 0.04 | 0.01 |
| Lifetime $L$ | 0.00 | 0.04 | 0.00 | 0.00 |
| Recordable $h$ | 0.00 | 0.01 | 0.02 | 0.00 |
| Hazard $\lambda$ | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.00 | 0.08 |

**Interpretation.** The question the field argues about most, *when will a CRQC exist?*, explains **3–17%** of the uncertainty in the quantum cost. The two economic inputs (how much value, and how much of it is new each year) explain **73–91%**. For assets with 30–50-year lifetimes the arrival date barely matters because $F_Q(L)$ has saturated near 1.

**Limit.** Because $V$ and $\tau$ enter *multiplicatively* and their assumed ranges (±0.5 and ±0.48 decades) are comparable, they are additive in $\log_{10}$ and share the variance by construction. The shares depend on the ranges chosen; a much wider CRQC range would raise its share. The one-at-a-time "tornado" (in `results.json`) also ranks loss and turnover as the two most influential inputs. Figure: Figure 10 (`python scripts/make_figures.py`).

## 5. Lifetime and the size of the quantum term, pooled across systems

**Question.** Does the quantum share of an asset's cost increase with its confidentiality lifetime, as the model predicts?

**Method.** Spearman's rank correlation between lifetime and quantum share across all 35 assets (Spearman 1904), with
* a **cluster bootstrap** (resampling whole systems, 10,000 times) for the interval, because assets within one system are not independent;
* a **within-system permutation** test (permuting shares within each system, 20,000 times) for the $p$-value, so that between-system differences cannot masquerade as the effect.

**Result.** $\rho_s=0.71$; cluster-bootstrap 95% interval **[0.47, 0.89]** (4 clusters); within-system permutation $p=1\times10^{-4}$. Figure: Figure 14 (`python scripts/make_figures.py`).

**Interpretation, with the necessary caveat.** This association is **largely built into the model** (Proposition 5: the quantum term rises with $L$; Proposition 6: it is zero for zero lifetime). It confirms that the implementation behaves as the theory predicts and that the case-study assets were assigned lifetimes in a way that exposes the effect. It is **not** an empirical discovery about real systems. With four clusters the bootstrap interval is necessarily wide.

## 6. Monte Carlo convergence

**Question.** Are 4,000 draws enough?

**Method.** The estimated probability and its 95% Wilson interval at 250, 500, … 16,000 draws.

**Result.** For O08 the estimate settles at 0.878 with a half-width of 0.005 at 16,000 draws (0.010 at 4,000). Figure: Figure 15 (`python scripts/make_figures.py`). The standard error falls as $1/\sqrt n$, as expected.

## 7. Numerical validation of the closed form

Two independent checks (details in `FORMAL_METHOD.md` §4 and Figure 11 (`python scripts/make_figures.py`)): (i) a Monte Carlo of the two competing clocks agrees with the closed form on 46 asset-by-scenario cases within three standard errors; (ii) the integration-by-parts quadrature agrees with adaptive quadrature to a worst relative error of $4.5\times10^{-10}$, against $9.4\times10^{-6}$ for direct Simpson integration; the measured convergence orders are 2.4 versus 1.4.

## 8. Scheduling analysis: does one list really beat two?

**Question.** If a team executes the work back to back, how much accrued loss does ranking by cost of delay avoid?

**Method.** Build a backlog with one *classical* item and one *quantum* item per asset (`costofdelay/schedule.py`). Each takes one year of team time. The quantum item accrues loss at its **exact time-varying rate** (it rises as a CRQC becomes more likely); the classical item at a constant rate. Compare: (a) one list ranked by cost of delay (Smith's rule, Proposition 13); (b) two queues, classical first; (c) two queues, quantum first; (d) the **exact optimum** found by dynamic programming over subsets.

**Result (committed loss, USD millions; ratio to the exact optimum in brackets).**

| System | Items | One list (Smith) | Classical first | Quantum first | Exact optimum |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| MOSIP | 16 | 10.81 (1.05) | 23.31 (2.27) | 14.00 (1.36) | 10.29 |
| Apache Fineract | 15 | 16.93 (1.06) | 25.93 (1.62) | 32.75 (2.05) | 15.97 |
| OpenMRS | 17 | 30.75 (1.06) | 40.00 (1.38) | 36.84 (1.27) | 28.96 |
| Online Boutique | 11 | 2.49 (1.10) | 5.67 (2.50) | 3.43 (1.51) | 2.27 |

**Interpretation.** One list ranked by cost of delay is within 5–10% of the true optimum on every system; keeping classical and quantum work in separate queues costs between 27% (OpenMRS, quantum first) and 150% (Online Boutique, classical first) more than the exact optimum under these assumptions. Proposition 14 gives the exact price of any sub-optimal order.

**Limit, and it is an important one.** This is a **model-internal** result: it says the ranking is optimal *for the model's own loss definition*. It does not show that real organisations accrue loss this way, and it uses assumed unit durations and one team. Smith's rule is exact only for constant rates; the time-varying quantum rate is why a gap to the optimum remains.

## 9. Comparison with the closest published score (QARS)

Kendall $\tau$ between the cost-of-delay ranking and a ranking by QARS (Grigaliūnas & Brūzgienė 2025; implemented from their equations with equal weights and the paper's linear timeline term): MOSIP 0.83, Fineract 0.67, OpenMRS 0.64, Online Boutique 0.71. Verifiable differences, each pinned by a unit test: an asset **already on ML-KEM still scores 0.67 ("high")** because QARS's gate zeroes only its exposure term; two assets differing only in classical hazard score identically, because QARS has no classical input; a tenfold change in asset value within a sensitivity band leaves its score unchanged. This is *our reading of the published equations*, which reproduce the paper's own Table 2 (0.733 and 0.383); it is not the authors' code, and it does not show that either score is more accurate.

## 10. Where the ordering changes

* **MOSIP:** invariant across the four CRQC scenarios (2032–2045); **changes with the discount rate** (at $\rho\ge5\%$ the demographic store M02 overtakes the registration packets M03).
* **OpenMRS:** changes across CRQC scenarios *and* discount rates. Off-site backups (O08) outrank the application-to-database link (O09) if the CRQC median is before about 2039 or if $\rho<0.7\%$.
* **Apache Fineract and Online Boutique:** invariant to both.

These boundaries, rather than any single ordering, are the decision-relevant uncertainty (Figure 8 (`python scripts/make_figures.py`)).

## 11. What was not done

* No test on outcome data: none exists linking these scores to realised harm.
* No hierarchical or Bayesian model: with four systems and judged inputs it would be over-parameterised.
* No inter-rater agreement: one analyst (assisted by a language model) populated every register.
* Multiplicity: the analyses are descriptive and were specified before inspection except where noted; no $p$-value correction is applied, and none is used for a headline claim.
